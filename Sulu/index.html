HISTORY

SULU


The following text/information are lifted from the book of Lee W. Vance entitled Tracing your Philippine Ancestors which was published in Provo, Utah by Stevenson’s Genealogical Center in 1980 and Symbols of the State by the Department of Interior and Local Government of the Republic of the Philippines in 1998.

 

The province of Sulu, comprising the northern half of the Sulu Archipelago, is located in the southernmost part of the Philippines.  It is located between the Sulu Sea on the north and west, and the Celebes Sea to the south and east.  It comprises some 2,600 scattered and isolated islands, stretching southwesterly from Mindanao’s Zamboanga peninsula to the northeastern coast of the Malay island of Borneo.

Sulu’s history antedates that of the rest of the Philippine Islands by many centuries.  Jolo, its capital, was a rich and well-known commercial center when the rest of the settlements of the country were still tiny frontier settlements.  Sulu comprised an independent political state and Muslim sultanate from its earliest history, being well known to merchants and sovereigns from Arabia to Japan.  It evidently had established commercial relations with China by the 13th Century.

Following is a time line1,2 of events that led to the creation and development of the province of Sulu:

DATE

E V E N T
  • 1375

     Mohammedanism was established in the region.  Makdum, a noted Arab scholar, visited Sulu and other islands of the Sulu Archipelago and introduced the Islam faith.
  • Early 15th Century

     Raja Baginda, an Arabian prince, settled in Bevansa, where he was joined by Abu Bakr, another noted Mohammedan missionary.
He established Islam as the state religion of Sulu and divided the sultanate into five administrative districts, each under a panglima.  He also revised the laws and published a new code which was adopted throughout the sultanate.
He encouraged commerce and established trade relations with China and the countries of Southeast Asia.

  • 1450

     Abu Bakr ascended to the throne of the Sultanate having married Parasimuli, the daughter of Rajah Baginda.
  • Early 17th Century

     The Spanish government made five major attempts to subdue the people of Sulu, and end the devastating Moro raids on Spanish settlements.  The first of these expeditions, all of which were ultimately unsuccessful, was launched in 1602 under the command of General Gallinato; the second, headed by Cristobal de Lugo, in 1628, the third by Olaso Ochotegui, in 1630, the fourth by Governor-General Corcuera in 1638 and the fifth by General Pedro de Almonte in 1639.
  • 1646

     The Spanish abandoned Sulu after attempt to hold the fort against the persistent attacks of the hostile Moros.
  • 1737

     Sultan Alimud Din I ascended to Sulu’s throne.  He began his progressive administration by revising Sulu’s laws and judicial system, and had the more important parts of the Koran translated into the Sulu tongue as well as several Arabic texts on religion and law and prepared an Arabic-Sulu Vocabulary, so that the people might learn to read and write the Arabic Language. 

  • Early 19th Century

     The British attempted to establish themselves in Sulu, but were unsuccessful. 

  • 1850

     The expedition organized by Governor-General Urbiztondo to subject Sulu for the second time were successful.  
The Sultanate was declared to be officially a “protectorate”.  The Sultan, agreed to the erection of a Spanish trading post at Jolo and the establishment of a small garrison to protect it, among other concessions made.
  • 1876

     A second expedition was sent to Sulu under General Malcamp who established a large garrison.
Captain Cervera was declared the governor of the newly-created politico-military province of Sulu.
  • 1884

     Civil war broke in Sulu due to the dispute over the succession to the thron of the Sultanate between Datu Alimud Din, Raja Muda and Datu Harun.  This lasted for ten years.
  • 1894

     Amirul Kiram became the Sultan of Sulu after an agreement among the contenders to the throne of the Sultanate.
  • 1899

     Spanish citizens were evacuated from Sulu in May of 1899, and the provincial government was turned over to the American military.  General John C. Bates negotiated a treaty with the Sultan of Sulu, guaranteeing Muslim and American Harmonious co-existence.  The Bates Treaty, or Treaty of Sulu, signed in August of the same year, recognized the sovereignty of the Sultan, allowed the free exercise of the Islamic religion, and respect for Islamic traditions.
  • June 1, 1903

     Sulu became one of the five districts of the newly created Moro Province.  Pursuant to Pilippine Commission Act 787, the district capital of Sulu was at Jolo, but the provincial capital was at Zamboanga. 

  • 1914

     Philippine Legislative Act 2408, approved July 23, 1914 dissolved the Moro Province and placing Sulu under the jurisdiction of the succeeding Department of Mindanao and Sulu.
  • 1915

     The sultan of Sulu abdicated his right of sovereignty, agreeing to retain only his right to be the head of the Moslem religion in the jurisdiction of the old Sultanate. 

  • April 1944

     The Sultanate was abolished as a political entity and Sulu became a part of the Philippine Commonwealth.
  • 1947

     The government succeeded in acquiring the islands of Taganak, Bakkaungan, Bayuna, Sibaung and Lihiman.  The islands were previously leased by the Sultan of Sulu to the British North Borneo Company government as part of the Borneo Agreement a century earlier.
  • Sept. 27, 1973

The southern half of the Sulu Archipelago was detached and organized as the province of Tawi-Tawi by virtue of Presidential Decree No. 302.
  • 2010

Sulu is now made up of the municipalities of Jolo, Marunggas, Indanan, Pangutaran, Parang, Talipao, Maimbung, Patikul, Kalingalan Calauang, Luuk, Old Panamao, Pata, Siasi, Tapul, Tongkil, New Panamao, Lugus, and Pandami.
